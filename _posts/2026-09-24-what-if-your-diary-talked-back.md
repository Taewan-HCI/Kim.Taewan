---
title: "What if your diary talked back?"
date: 2026-09-24
tags: [research]
---

Most diaries are one-directional. You write, the page absorbs it, and that's the whole transaction. A few years ago, before ChatGPT existed and before there was any real precedent for what "AI-assisted journaling" should even look like, a few of us started wondering what happens if the page writes back.

That question became **DiaryMate**, a small journaling prototype my collaborators and I built in 2022. We weren't trying to ship a product. We were using it as what HCI researchers call a *technology probe*: a rough, working thing dropped into people's real lives, built less to solve a problem than to see what happens when you hand people something genuinely new.

The probe did two things. If you got stuck mid-entry, it could offer a next sentence, gently continuing whatever thread you'd started. Or you could hand it a few keywords (a feeling, a person, a place) and let it draft a line around them. Either way, the suggestion just sat there quietly until you decided whether to use it, edit it, or ignore it entirely.

We ran it as a ten-day field study with 28 people. One pattern stood out: people who spent more time *browsing* the AI's suggestions (reading them, weighing them, not necessarily accepting them) tended to write longer entries. Just seeing an alternative seemed to be doing something on its own, independent of whether they actually used it.

But "something" cuts both ways. One participant told us:

> "I want to see myself more objectively. It was a meaningful experience to see what other people might think about the exact same keywords." (P10)

Reading a stranger's-eye view of your own week, generated instantly, can genuinely loosen something open. But another participant described the opposite pull:

> "I had my own thoughts and emotions. But after I read AI's suggestions, I forgot my feelings and followed what AI said." (P15)

That's the part I keep coming back to. The same mechanism that can hand you a new angle on yourself can just as easily talk over you: quietly replacing a half-formed, honest sentence with a fluent, plausible one that was never really yours. A journal is supposed to be evidence of what you actually thought. If the suggestion is good enough, you might not notice it's become the author.

I don't think the answer is "don't use AI to journal." I think it's that reflection tools deserve to be judged on more than whether they produce more writing. The real question is whose voice ends up on the page. We published the full study at CHI 2024; the numbers and the rest of the interviews are in the paper. But that tension is the part that's stayed with me since.
